# International, third-party, and station location rules

An operator's home certificate is only part of a travel check. Where the station stands decides whose limits apply. The message must also fit amateur purposes, the people must have the right permission, and the identification must match the visit arrangement.

Use these principles for study. Before real travel or maritime operation, confirm the current official rules for the destination and arrangement.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you can:

- connect a station's location to the applicable frequency limits;
- keep international messages personal or related to amateur radio;
- supervise a non-certified guest during local and international contacts;
- check visiting-operator permission; and
- distinguish United States and CEPT voice-identification order.

## Reference facts: Location decides the applicable limits

The International Telecommunication Union (ITU) provides the international radio-regulation framework. Its regions help organize the map. A region label does not replace the rules for the country or waters where the station operates.

| Location or framework | Rule | Why |
|---|---|---|
| International framework | Canadian amateurs must follow the ITU Radio Regulations as well as Canadian requirements. | Radio use crosses national borders. |
| Canada | In ITU Region 2, which includes the Americas. | Link the number to its part of the map. |
| Europe and Africa | In ITU Region 1. | Region numbers are geographic labels. |
| Australia, Japan and Southeast Asia | In ITU Region 3. | Keep this group distinct from the Americas. |
| A Canadian operator in Florida | United States amateur band limits apply. | The station is operating in the United States. |
| A Canadian operator 7 km off Florida | United States amateur band limits still apply. | That position is inside United States territorial waters. |

The United States territorial sea extends 12 nautical miles, about 22 km. Seven kilometres is about 3.8 nautical miles, so going that far offshore does not put the station outside it.

## Reference facts: International permission includes purpose

International amateur contacts carry amateur-purpose or personal messages. A national administration can object to contacts; good intentions do not cancel that objection.

| International check | Rule | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Message purpose | The operator must keep foreign contacts to amateur-radio purposes or personal remarks. | A border does not turn amateur radio into a business service. |
| Administration objection | An administration can bar the contact by filing an objection with the ITU; without one, international communication is allowed. | The other country's permission matters too. |
| Morse authorization | Each administration decides whether Morse proficiency is required for authorization. | A national requirement is not a universal ITU test requirement. |

## Reference facts: A guest speaks under supervision

Third-party communication is carried on behalf of someone without amateur certification. The third party can be beside you; the message does not have to come from a distant organization.

| People or traffic | Rule | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Third-party purpose | Keep it personal and non-commercial. | The service remains amateur even when helping someone else. |
| Canadian third-party service | May be provided with any other amateur station, including internationally where the administration has not objected. | Canada allows international third-party traffic; check the other administration. |
| Emergency or disaster-relief third-party traffic | International use is expressly permitted unless the foreign administration specifically prohibits it. | Retain the foreign-administration condition even for relief. |
| A non-certified guest at your station | Keep monitoring and supervising, including when a foreign station joins the contact. | You remain responsible; the foreign contact also needs the permission check. |
| Non-certified local participants at both stations | Both are third parties. | Count people by certification, not which end they occupy. |

The unpaid-message rule in L-001-03 still applies. A personal message does not become a paid service because it crosses a border.

## Reference facts: Visit arrangements pair permission with identification

CEPT, the European Conference of Postal and Telecommunications Administrations, provides a visit arrangement used by participating countries. Keep its qualification and call-sign order together. The United States arrangement has a different minimum qualification and order.

| Visit situation | Permission or identification rule | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Foreign visitor to Canada, other than a United States citizen | Needs an agreement between the countries and the appropriate permit. | A home qualification alone is not the whole visit check. |
| CEPT amateur licence holder in Canada | ISED grants Advanced privileges. | Match this arrangement to its Canadian privilege level. |
| Canadian visiting the United States | Basic is the minimum Canadian qualification. | This is a separate arrangement from CEPT. |
| Canadian using voice in the United States | Canadian call sign first; portable or mobile next; visited US call-area prefix last. | In this arrangement, the home call leads. |
| Canadian using a CEPT Amateur Radio Licence abroad | Advanced is the minimum Canadian qualification. | Pair CEPT access with Advanced. |
| Canadian using voice under CEPT | Host-country prefix first, then "stroke", then the Canadian call sign. | In this arrangement, the host prefix leads. |
| United States-issued call sign used in Canada | Give the city and province at least once in the contact. | The call sign alone does not state the Canadian location. |
| United States visitor using voice in Canada | Home call sign, then mobile or portable, then the Canadian geographic prefix. | Like the Canadian visit to the US, the home call leads. |

## How these rules fit together

Begin with location, then check purpose and permission. ITU regions orient the map, while the station's actual country or territorial waters determine the applicable limits. Check whether the destination arrangement recognizes your qualifications.

Next check the people and message. Supervising a guest continues when a foreign station joins. Canada permits third-party traffic, but a foreign administration's objection still matters.

Finish with identification. In the United States arrangement, remember "home call first". Under CEPT, remember "host prefix first". Keep that study cue with the full sequence in the table; do not treat it as a substitute for checking current visit rules.

## Radio connection

### In the real world: one travel card

Before packing the radio, write down the current operating location, visit permission, band limits and identification order. Add the third-party conditions if a guest will use the microphone.

Keep that card with the headset and power lead. Check it again when crossing a border or moving offshore. A well-built portable station still needs permission for the place where it is used.

## Summary

Location sets the operating context; purpose and permission decide whether the contact fits. Supervision continues across borders. Pair each visit arrangement with its own qualification and identification order rather than using one travel rule everywhere.
