# VHF, UHF, and unusual propagation modes

VHF and UHF are usually local, but "usually" is not "always." The lower atmosphere can bend or scatter signals. Dense patches near the E region can open six metres. Aurora and meteor trails can create brief or distorted paths. These modes are exciting because they break the everyday range expectation, not because they are dependable replacements for ordinary coverage.

This lesson focuses on clues: what band is involved, what the signal sounds like, what weather or solar condition may be present, and what operating style fits the opening.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you can:

- describe sporadic E and why six metres is the classic amateur band for it;
- explain tropospheric bending, ducting, and scatter on VHF/UHF;
- recognize auroral propagation and why CW often survives distortion better than voice FM;
- explain how meteor scatter produces short VHF opportunities; and
- distinguish weak scatter from ordinary ground-wave, sky-wave, or line-of-sight paths.

## Sporadic E and six metres

Sporadic E is a patchy, dense ionization condition at E-region height. It can refract signals that normally would not return from that region. The six-metre band is famous for this: on an ordinary day it behaves much like a local VHF band, but during a sporadic-E opening it can produce contacts far outside normal line-of-sight range.

The word sporadic is important. These openings can appear quickly, cover a surprising area, and disappear just as quickly. Operators often watch beacon clusters, call frequencies, and signal reports to notice the opening while it lasts.

### Worked example: a sudden six-metre map

A station hears no distant signals on six metres at lunch. Later in the afternoon, several stations from a single region appear with strong but changing signals. The local VHF repeater coverage has not changed. A good working explanation is a sporadic-E patch affecting six metres, not a permanent improvement in the station antenna.

## Tropospheric bending, ducting, and scatter

The troposphere is the lower atmosphere where weather happens. Normal refraction slightly extends the radio horizon. Without an enhanced mode, VHF tropospheric communication is still limited; use roughly 800 km as the broad upper planning scale for normal VHF tropospheric range, not as a guaranteed daily path. Stronger gradients in temperature, humidity, and pressure can bend VHF and UHF signals more than usual. A temperature inversion can form a duct, trapping part of the wave so it travels much farther than expected.

Tropospheric scatter is different from ducting. It uses small variations in lower-atmosphere density and water-vapour content to redirect a small amount of energy beyond the radio horizon. Scattering sends small pieces of a wave off in many directions rather than bending the whole wave smoothly. Signals are often weak, but the mode can explain contacts that are beyond ordinary line of sight without requiring an ionospheric opening.

Accessible text formula: ordinary VHF range starts with antenna height and radio horizon; enhanced tropospheric propagation adds bending, ducting, or scatter when lower-atmosphere conditions help. A path greatly beyond the normal scale is a clue to look for enhancement such as a duct.

### Worked example: lake-path ducting

After a calm evening, a cool air layer settles over a large lake while warmer air sits above it. A coastal station suddenly hears distant VHF repeaters across the water. The path follows the weather boundary for a while, so the opening is strongest along that route and may vanish when the inversion mixes out.

## Aurora, meteor scatter, and distorted signals

Auroral propagation uses disturbed ionization near E-region height in high-latitude directions. In the northern hemisphere, operators often aim antennas northward to use the auroral region. The reflected or scattered signal can sound raspy or fluttery because the aurora is moving and irregular. CW is often more readable than analog voice through that distortion.

Meteor scatter uses brief ionized trails left by meteors. VHF energy can reflect or scatter from the trail for a short burst. It is most effective for extended-range work from about 30 MHz to 100 MHz: below that range ordinary ionospheric propagation, absorption, and noise tend to dominate, while much higher frequencies make the trail less effective. Six metres sits in that useful range, and specialized digital or fast-exchange techniques can make use of very short openings.

HF scatter can put weak, distorted signals into a skip zone. Only a small part of the transmitted energy is redirected toward the receiver, and multiple paths can smear the signal.

### Worked example: choosing the mode for a rough auroral path

A VHF operator hears signals from the north with a harsh, buzzing tone. FM voice is difficult to understand, but short Morse characters can still be copied. The operator switches to a mode and message style that tolerate distortion instead of assuming the receiver has failed.

## Recognizing the clues

Use the band and the symptom together:

- Six metres with sudden distant stations: suspect sporadic E, meteor scatter, or aurora depending on timing and direction.
- Two metres or higher with distant coastal or flatland paths during stable weather: suspect tropospheric enhancement or ducting.
- Weak, distorted HF from a normally skipped-over area: suspect scatter near the usable-frequency limit.
- Very brief VHF bursts: suspect meteor scatter, especially during known shower periods.
- Raspy VHF signals from a northern direction: suspect auroral propagation in northern-hemisphere operating.

Do not turn these clues into guarantees. The same station may need a higher antenna for ordinary range, a different band for an HF path, or simply patience for the next opening.

## Try it

1. A six-metre opening appears suddenly and then vanishes after twenty minutes. What operating habit follows from that timing? Keep exchanges short, log quickly, and keep monitoring beacons because the patch can move or fade before a long conversation finishes.
2. A two-metre contact reaches just beyond the normal planning scale during a calm evening over water. What evidence should the operator record before calling it a new everyday range? Note the weather layering, antenna direction, time, and distance so the contact can be tied to enhanced lower-atmosphere conditions rather than assumed permanent coverage.
3. During a northern VHF opening, a club hears raspy signals but needs only short call-sign and grid exchanges. What operating style fits the clue? Use a narrow, simple mode and short messages that tolerate rapid flutter and distortion.
4. A marginal HF path produces intelligible peaks followed by smeared audio on the same frequency. What should the operator log before blaming the radio? Record the frequency, time, signal strength, and distortion pattern so mixed propagation can be compared with later openings.
5. A meteor-shower schedule uses fast digital bursts instead of long voice overs. Explain the timing choice. The ionized trails last briefly, so short structured messages have a better chance of fitting into each reflection.

## Radio connection

Unusual propagation rewards listening. Keep a memory channel or receiver slice on local beacons, note weather patterns, and compare what you hear with the direction of your antenna. When an opening starts, use concise exchanges and suitable modes; the path may be short-lived.

For station planning, do not rely on unusual modes for routine safety coverage. Use them as opportunities for learning and contacts, while ordinary communication plans still depend on repeaters, line-of-sight paths, HF nets, or other dependable systems.

## Common mistakes

- Assuming every distant VHF signal is ionospheric skip.
- Treating sporadic E as predictable daily coverage.
- Confusing tropospheric ducting with meteor scatter just because both can extend VHF range.
- Expecting wideband FM voice to sound clean on an auroral path.
- Forgetting that scatter signals are often weak because only a small portion of energy reaches the receiver.

## Summary

- Sporadic E is patchy E-region ionization and is especially important on six metres.
- Tropospheric bending slightly extends VHF/UHF range; ordinary VHF tropospheric range is roughly limited to an 800 km planning scale unless enhancement is present.
- Tropospheric scatter redirects a small amount of VHF/UHF energy beyond the normal horizon.
- Auroral propagation is often distorted; CW is usually more readable than analog voice FM.
- Meteor scatter uses brief ionized trails and is most useful from about 30 MHz to 100 MHz, including six metres.
- Scattering redirects parts of a wave in many directions, so received signals are often weak or smeared.
- Unusual modes are opportunities, not guaranteed everyday coverage.
