# Morse, RST, and Q signals

Morse and voice contacts use many of the same operating ideas: call clearly, identify, leave space, and report what you actually hear. Morse adds short procedural signals that save time when every character is being sent by hand or keyer.

This lesson focuses on the abbreviations and signal-report habits the Basic bank expects: CQ, DE, K, R, DX, 73, QSK, RST reports, selected Q signals, and minimum separation for Morse contacts.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you can:

- make and answer a routine Morse CQ at a speed you can copy;
- distinguish CQ, DE, K, R, DX, 73, and full break-in QSK;
- read two-number voice reports and three-number Morse RST reports;
- use the S-unit relationship for simple power changes; and
- recognize the common Q signals in this module.

## Morse calling basics

A Morse CQ is a general call. In routine form, it sends CQ, gives DE to mean "from," sends the caller's call sign, and then listens. The point is not to win a speed contest. Send at a speed you can receive, because the answering station is likely to reply near that speed.

When answering a routine Morse CQ, identify the station you are calling and then yourself. The exchange is compact, but it still follows the same courtesy as voice: make it clear who is being called and who is calling.

### Worked example: choosing a sensible speed

Sam can send clean code at 18 words per minute but reliably copies new call signs at 12. For a first CQ on a quiet band, Sam sends near 12. That choice invites a reply Sam can actually understand instead of creating a fast contact that collapses after the first exchange.

## Short Morse signals and abbreviations

Several short groups appear often:

| Signal | Practical meaning in routine amateur use |
|---|---|
| CQ | A general call for any station. |
| DE | From; it separates the called station or CQ from the caller's call sign. |
| K | Invitation for a reply from any station. |
| R | All received. |
| DX | A distant station or distant contact. |
| 73 | Best regards. |
| QSK | Full break-in CW, where receive audio returns between transmitted elements. |

Full break-in is a station capability and operating style. With QSK, an operator may hear another station between the dots and dashes being sent. That is different from using a manual send/receive switch or asking someone to interrupt by sending a break signal.

Morse contacts are narrow compared with voice, but they still need spacing. The Basic bank teaches 150 Hz to 500 Hz as the minimum separation to allow from a CW contact in progress.

## Signal reports: RS and RST

Signal reports describe reception at your station. For voice, operators normally use readability and strength: two numbers such as 5 7. For Morse, the report adds tone quality: readability, strength, and tone, often written as RST.

Readability tells how understandable the signal is. Strength describes relative received signal level. Tone applies to a keyed carrier and is most meaningful for Morse.

Useful anchors from the Basic bank include:

- 5 7: perfectly readable and moderately strong;
- 3 3: readable with considerable difficulty and weak;
- 1 1: unreadable and barely perceptible;
- 579: perfectly readable, moderately strong, and perfect tone;
- 459: quite readable, fair strength, and perfect tone.

Reports should describe the signal you actually copy. Through a repeater, report the station's signal and audio as it comes through the repeater, not merely your S-meter reading of the repeater output.

### Worked example: separating readability from strength

A station is low on the meter but has clean audio and no missing words. You might give a good readability number with a modest strength number. A different station may be loud but distorted or covered by noise; strength alone does not guarantee readability.

## S-units and power changes

Some receiver S-meters are calibrated with 6 dB per S-unit. That step corresponds to a quadrupling or quartering of transmitter power in the simplified exam model. Therefore, under the condition that propagation and antennas do not change, one S-unit can represent one such power step.

### Worked example: one S-unit lower

Suppose a local test signal reads S9 at 90 W on a receiver using the 6 dB per S-unit convention. To estimate the power for S8 with everything else unchanged, quarter the transmitter power. The estimate is 22.5 W. Real meters and real propagation are not perfect, but the exam relationship is the same power-ratio step.

## Q signals in this module

Q signals are three-letter operating abbreviations. In Morse they can be sent as questions or statements. A question mark changes the job: QRL? asks whether a frequency is busy; QRL without the question mark can state that it is busy.

The Basic bank uses these meanings:

| Q signal | Meaning taught in this module |
|---|---|
| QRL? | Is this frequency in use? |
| QRZ? | Who is calling me? |
| QRS | Send more slowly. |
| QSY | Change frequency. |
| QSB | Your signal is fading. |
| QRM | I am being interfered with. |
| QRN | I am troubled by static. |
| QRX | I will call you again. |
| QTH | My location is ... |

Use Q signals when they make communication clearer. On voice, plain language is often better unless both operators expect the abbreviation.

## Try it

1. A station answers your CQ much faster than you can copy. What concise request fits the lesson? Send QRS or ask for slower sending, then resume at a speed you can receive accurately.
2. You copied “VA3AAA DE VE5BBB” but missed the rest. Which station was transmitting? VE5BBB was transmitting, because DE marks the call sign that the message is from.
3. A voice station is easy to understand but not especially strong. Which report part should be high and which should be moderate? Readability can be high while strength is only moderate.
4. Your S-meter jumps upward during deep QSB on the band. Why should you avoid calculating the other station's power from that change? The path changed, so the simple S-unit power relationship no longer has its controlled assumption.
5. You send QRL? and hear a partial reply under noise. What is the careful next step? Keep listening or ask again briefly before calling CQ, because a fragment may mean the frequency is occupied.

## Radio connection

Morse procedure rewards listening discipline. A clean QRL? before calling, a speed you can copy, and a realistic signal report all help contacts succeed with very little transmitted energy.

RST reports also train you to separate receiver impressions. Readability, strength, and tone are different observations. A good operator reports the path as heard, not what they hope the other station wants to hear.

## Common mistakes

- Sending a CQ faster than you can copy the answer.
- Treating DE as a person's initials instead of the procedural word "from."
- Using a three-number RST report on voice without thinking about whether tone applies.
- Reporting the strength of a repeater output when the other station asked about audio through the repeater.
- Confusing QRM from other signals with QRN from static.
- Forgetting that QRL? is a question before transmitting, not a decoration at the end of a call.

## Summary

- Morse CQ is a general call; DE means from; K invites a reply; R means all received.
- Send CQ at a speed you can receive reliably.
- DX means distant station, and 73 means best regards.
- RST reports describe received readability, strength, and, for Morse, tone.
- With 6 dB per S-unit, one S-unit represents a quadrupled or quartered power ratio in the simplified exam model.
- Q signals such as QRL?, QRZ?, QRS, QSY, QSB, QRM, QRN, QRX, and QTH are compact operating tools.
