# Distress, urgency, and safety traffic

Emergency operating begins with calm priority decisions. The Basic bank establishes a small set of facts for this module: MAYDAY or SOS are for life-threatening distress, a remote request for help without immediate risk to life is urgent, safety messages have lower priority than distress, and a station that hears distress traffic should help or keep listening while proper authorities are notified.

This lesson does not add unverified distress-word systems. It teaches only the terms and priorities established by the official Basic answers and keeps the focus on practical amateur conduct.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you can:

- distinguish distress from urgent and safety-message traffic using the Basic-bank principles;
- state the voice distress signal and Morse distress signal taught in the bank;
- interrupt a repeater conversation in a distress situation without adding confusion;
- decide what to do after hearing distress traffic; and
- list simple station preparations for communication during a power outage.

## Priority starts with risk

Distress is the highest priority in this lesson. The Basic bank ties SOS or MAYDAY use to a life-threatening distress situation. In voice, the distress word taught by the bank is MAYDAY, transmitted three times. In Morse code, the distress signal taught by the bank is SOS.

Urgent traffic is serious but not the same as immediate danger to life. The bank's example classifies a request for assistance from an isolated place, when life is not immediately at risk, as urgent.

Safety messages are also part of the priority ladder. The bank establishes that distress traffic comes before safety-message traffic. It also establishes that distress traffic comes before emergency-message traffic. For exam purposes, do not demote distress just because another message is official-sounding or scheduled.

### Worked example: sorting message priority

A club net hears three possible items: a routine equipment-sale announcement, a report that a hiker is overdue but in phone contact with family, and a station reporting a life-threatening injury. The life-threatening report is handled first. The overdue-hiker item may be important, but it does not outrank distress in this lesson's priority model.

## Breaking into routine traffic

If you are in distress on a repeater that is already busy, break in between transmissions. State your call sign and the situation. Do not bury the essential information under repeated shouting. The operators already using the repeater need enough facts to understand that the routine conversation must stop and assistance is needed.

If you are one of the routine stations and you hear a distress break-in, acknowledge the distressed station. Determine location and what assistance may be needed. If you cannot directly assist, notify the appropriate authorities and keep listening in case your station becomes useful.

### Worked example: a clear interruption

Two stations are discussing antennas through a repeater. During a pause, a third station gives a call sign and says there is a medical emergency on a trail. The right response is not to continue the antenna chat or change frequency. One station acknowledges the emergency station, asks for location and needed assistance, and another operator contacts appropriate help while the repeater remains available.

## Prepared stations are useful stations

An emergency is a poor time to discover that every battery is flat. The Basic bank emphasizes two simple readiness ideas: preparing to operate without commercial AC power lets a station communicate during a power outage, and handheld emergency communication depends on an adequate supply of charged batteries.

Portable HF equipment may use simple antennas that can be carried and erected without a tower. The bank identifies a dipole or a vertical as good portable HF choices for emergency setup. That does not make every emergency an HF problem; it means the antenna choice is practical when HF is the tool being deployed.

## Keep records during priority traffic

Even though this module later teaches logs for routine contacts, emergency notes deserve special care. Write times, call signs, locations, frequencies, names if given, requested assistance, and the action you took. Notes reduce memory errors when you relay details to authorities or another station.

Use plain language when it prevents confusion. Abbreviations are useful only if every station involved understands them under stress.

## Try it

1. A distressed operator gives a situation but forgets to give a call sign. What should you ask for after acknowledging them? Ask for call sign, location, and needed assistance so the relay can be acted on.
2. You must pass emergency details to authorities by telephone. What information belongs in your first note? Record the time, frequency, call sign if known, location, and requested help before memory starts filling gaps.
3. A handheld go-kit has one partly used pack installed and a charged spare in the case. Which pack should start the deployment? Start with the charged pack and keep the partly used pack as backup, because runtime is part of communication reliability.
4. A portable HF antenna is packed, but no support line is in the bag. What non-radio item should be added? Add suitable cord or support hardware so the antenna can actually be erected when needed.
5. During emergency traffic, why write the location exactly as received? Written notes help prevent a later relay from changing the place, time, or needed assistance.

## Radio connection

A radio operator in an emergency is valuable because they can listen, prioritize, and relay accurately. Transmitter power is less important than calm procedure: clear the channel, collect the essential facts, pass them to someone who can help, and keep watch until released or no longer useful.

Emergency readiness is also ordinary station maintenance. Charged batteries, a known-good portable antenna, and a way to operate without commercial AC power turn a hobby station into a communication resource when other systems are unavailable.

## Common mistakes

- Using MAYDAY or SOS for non-distress situations.
- Treating urgent traffic as identical to life-threatening distress.
- Continuing a routine conversation after a distress break-in.
- Leaving the frequency immediately when you may be the only station that can copy the distressed operator.
- Packing a handheld radio for emergencies without charged spare batteries.
- Relying on memory instead of writing the details of priority traffic.

## Summary

- MAYDAY is the voice distress signal taught by the Basic bank, and SOS is the Morse distress signal.
- Distress is tied to life-threatening situations and outranks the other message classes in this lesson.
- A serious remote-help request without immediate life risk is urgent in the Basic-bank principle.
- Break into a repeater between transmissions with your call sign and situation when distress requires it.
- Acknowledge distress traffic, determine location and assistance needed, notify authorities if you cannot directly help, and keep listening.
- Station readiness includes non-AC power, charged batteries, and practical portable antennas.
