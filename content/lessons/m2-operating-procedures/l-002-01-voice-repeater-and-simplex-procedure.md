# Voice, repeater, and simplex procedure

Voice operating is courtesy made repeatable. The operator listens first, identifies clearly, chooses a suitable path, leaves room for others, and keeps the station adjusted so one contact uses only the spectrum and repeater time it needs.

This lesson stays with practical operating principles: repeater use, coordination, CTCSS access, the two-metre FM offset, the ITU phonetic alphabet, CQ and directed calls, simplex choices, band plans, dummy loads, and shared-frequency manners.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you can:

- call a known station on a repeater or voice frequency with a concise format;
- explain why repeaters have offsets, access tones, timers, and coordinated channels;
- spell a call sign with ITU phonetic words;
- decide when simplex is the better courtesy choice; and
- tune, test, and join nets while protecting contacts already under way.

## Repeaters are shared relays

A repeater receives on one frequency and retransmits on another. Portable and mobile stations often have small antennas, low height, and moving locations. A repeater at a better site can hear those stations and retransmit from a stronger location, increasing their practical local range.

Because many operators share the same relay, repeater procedure is deliberately short. Listen before transmitting. Leave a clear pause after another station unkeys so new traffic can enter. Keep overs brief enough that other users, including emergency traffic, can get access. Join a conversation already under way by identifying with your call sign during a clear break after the previous transmission ends.

A time-out timer is a protection device. It interrupts transmissions that run too long, including a stuck microphone. Courteous operating leaves pauses and keeps overs brief before the controller has to act.

## Coordination, tones, and offsets

Frequency coordination is the organized recommendation process used on VHF and UHF to reduce conflicts among neighbouring repeaters. It considers repeater channels, offset, coverage area, and local practice. A band plan is different: it is a voluntary division of an amateur band by compatible activities and modes.

Many repeaters require a CTCSS tone. The tone is a low-frequency access signal sent with the user's transmission so the repeater receiver opens its audio path only for signals carrying the expected tone. Successful access depends on matching the repeater input frequency, offset direction, and tone.

On the two-metre FM repeater channels covered here, the standard difference between transmit and receive frequencies is 600 kHz. Radios may show the repeater output while automatically transmitting above or below it by the programmed offset.

## Voice identification and phonetics

Call signs must be understood through noise, accents, flutter, and weak audio. The International Telecommunication Union phonetic alphabet gives one standard word for each letter. Use it when letters could be confused, and especially when repairing a missed suffix or spelling a complete call sign.

Standard phonetic words give every listener the same spelling system. On repeaters, ordinary plain-language questions keep location and status exchanges clear.

## Calling on voice

A general voice call invites any station. A directed voice call names one station. Keep those jobs separate so listeners immediately know whether the call is open to everyone or meant for a particular station.

For a directed call, put the desired station's call sign first and then identify your own station. For an answer to CQ, name the station whose CQ you are answering and then identify yourself. On repeaters, a short announcement with your call sign and listening status is friendlier to the shared machine.

### Reference fact: voice calling patterns

| Situation | Pattern to recognize |
|---|---|
| General voice CQ | Say CQ three times, then "this is," then your call sign three times. |
| Answering a voice CQ | Say the other station's call sign once, then "this is," then your call sign. |
| Directed voice call | Say the desired station's call sign, then "this is," then your call sign. |

The repetition in the CQ pattern gives listeners enough time to recognize a general call and still keeps the call sign close to the invitation.

## Simplex and repeater courtesy

Simplex means both stations transmit and receive on the same frequency. If two stations can hear each other directly, moving from a repeater to simplex can free the repeater for mobiles, portables, and wider-area traffic.

A useful simplex test while already using a repeater is to listen for the other station on the repeater's input frequency. If the input signal is clear at your station, direct communication may be practical. Choose the simplex frequency carefully. A channel that sounds quiet in your receiver may be the input to a local repeater; your transmitted signal can interfere with repeater users on that system. If you discover you are using a repeater channel for simplex, move to a proper simplex frequency.

For local communication, VHF and UHF are usually preferred over HF when they will do the job. That keeps HF bands less cluttered for the longer-distance communication for which they are especially useful.

## Sidebands, spacing, and clean tests

Two sideband conventions in this lesson are upper sideband on twenty metres and lower sideband around 3755 kHz. Operators use these conventions so signals line up as expected and have the expected audio orientation.

Before transmitting on any frequency, listen carefully. Listening is the first shared-channel action because it protects contacts already in progress. If propagation changes and interference grows during a contact, moving to another clear frequency is more courteous than demanding that everyone else move.

For lengthy transmitter tests, use a dummy load. A dummy load gives the transmitter a proper load and keeps test energy off the air by turning RF power into heat. For a brief antenna tuner adjustment before joining a sideband net on HF, tune at a nearby clear spot between three and five kilohertz from the active net channel, then return only when the net frequency is clear for use.

Single-sideband voice signals need room. Allow approximately 3 kHz from a contact already in progress. Morse contacts use much less bandwidth and are covered in the next lesson with narrower spacing.

## Nets, power, and band plans

A net shares its scheduled frequency with current users. If the normal net frequency is occupied before the net begins, the net control station should ask politely whether the occupants can accommodate the net. When the occupants are unable to accommodate it, the net should move to a clear frequency. If you are already using a frequency and a scheduled net is about to begin, the courteous action is to move if you can.

Courtesy includes power control. If the other station reports full readability and far more strength than needed, reduce transmitter power to the minimum that still maintains reliable communication. Lower power reduces unnecessary RF exposure, battery drain, and interference potential.

## How these procedures fit together

The sequence is simple: listen, choose the correct operating path, identify clearly, keep transmissions short, and leave room for other stations. Repeater offsets and tones make the relay work technically. Coordination and band plans reduce conflicts among users. Phonetics and plain language make the message intelligible. Dummy loads, spacing, and minimum power reduce unnecessary radiation. Each procedure protects the same shared resource: a usable amateur frequency.

## Radio connection

A repeater is a shared station with its own receiver, transmitter, controller, timer, access settings, antenna, and coordinated channel. Good operating makes all of those pieces useful to more people.

Simplex is the other half of local operating. A good amateur learns both habits: use the repeater when it adds coverage, and step away from it when direct communication is reliable.

## Summary

- Repeater procedure links technical access settings with short, courteous transmissions.
- Directed calls, CQ calls, and CQ answers each identify the intended communication path.
- Standard phonetics and plain language help listeners copy identification and location information.
- Simplex frees a repeater when the direct path is reliable.
- Listening first, using a dummy load for tests, respecting band plans, and controlling power all reduce unnecessary interference.
